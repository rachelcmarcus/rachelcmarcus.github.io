Resilience Analysis: Structural Disadvantage and Young-Adult Outcomes

Reproducible secondary analysis of the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health)

Author

Rachel C. Marcus, Ph.D.

Project Overview

This quantitative work sample demonstrates an end-to-end secondary-data analysis using the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health) (Harris 2013). The analysis was originally developed for the 21st Annual Sloboda and Bukoski SPR Cup and was selected as one of four national finalist projects presented at the Society for Prevention Research 34th Annual Meeting (Marcus et al. 2026).

Project at a Glance

Research question: Which individual, family, school, and community factors are associated with better-than-expected young-adult outcomes given structural disadvantage in adolescence?

Data: Add Health Waves I and III; analytic N = 4,882.

Methods: Secondary-data cleaning and harmonization; complex survey-weighted regression; outcome-specific beta-weighted structural disadvantage composites; residual-based continuous resilience scores; multivariable regression; exploratory subgroup comparisons and formal interaction tests.

Tools: R, SPSS, Quarto, Git/GitHub.

My role: Led the analytic design, operationalization, statistical analysis, interpretation, visualization, and reproducible documentation as part of a multidisciplinary research team.

The analysis proceeds in five steps:

Section 2: Wave I structural disadvantage indicators are used to build outcome-specific disadvantage composites (Marquez, Francis-Hew, and Humphrey 2023).

Section 3: Wave III outcomes are regressed on the corresponding disadvantage composites (Miller-Lewis et al. 2013).

Section 4: Standardized residuals from those models become continuous resilience scores: the degree to which a young adult is doing better or worse than expected given their adolescent disadvantage exposure.

Section 5: Multilevel resilience factors (individual, family, school, and community) are tested as predictors of resilience in the full sample.

Section 6: Exploratory low- and high-disadvantage subgroup estimates are paired with formal interaction tests to evaluate whether associations differ as disadvantage increases.

Data Preparation and Quality Assurance

Data preparation was completed in SPSS before import into R. Four domain-specific syntax files covered demographics, structural disadvantage, young-adult outcomes, and hypothesized resilience factors. The cleaning workflow was intentionally documented before modeling so that transformations could be audited independently of the R analysis.

Across the four domains, preparation included:

  • Demographics: recoding and QA for biological sex, age, and race/ethnicity variables used as covariates or structural-disadvantage indicators.
  • Structural disadvantage: construction of minority-status, school, economic-hardship, family socioeconomic, subjective-neighborhood, and census-neighborhood measures, including direction harmonization and standardization where needed.
  • Young-adult outcomes: construction of Wave III depression, substance-use, educational-attainment, and general-health measures.
  • Resilience factors: construction of community connectedness, community integration, optimism/hopefulness, self-esteem, self-efficacy, parent-child relationship, and school connectedness.
  • Quality assurance: inspection of source-variable frequencies, explicit data cleaning of special missing-value codes, reverse scoring, dummy coding, scale/composite construction, reliability checks, post-transformation frequency checks, and standardization within the valid weighted analytic sample.

Explicit Data Cleaning and Missing-Data Handling Example

Across the SPSS preprocessing files, source-variable frequencies were inspected before recoding so that valid responses, study-specific special codes, and legitimate skips could be handled explicitly rather than treated as equivalent values.

Demographic covariates, including biological sex, age, and race/ethnicity, were cleaned separately in Demographics.sps using the same pattern of source-frequency checks, explicit recoding, variable labels, and post-transformation validation.

The parental-employment component of family socioeconomic disadvantage provides an example of this process.

For both the responding parent and their partner, the source variables distinguish full-time employment (1), not employed full time (0), refusal (6), and a legitimate skip for respondents who do not work outside the home (7). Refusals are converted to system missing, whereas legitimate skips are coded as not employed full time because that response carries substantive information for the employment indicator. Frequencies are checked before and after the transformation. The two cleaned indicators are then averaged across available parents, allowing the composite to retain information when employment data are available for only one parent.

Young-Adult Outcomes

Outcomes.sps constructs the four Wave III outcomes used in the resilience models: depression, substance use, educational attainment, and general health. Substance use combines cleaned alcohol, cigarette, and marijuana-use indicators; the remaining outcomes are similarly recoded and checked before standardization.

Depressive symptoms provide a useful example of the full item-to-analysis-variable workflow. The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) was originally developed as a self-report measure of depressive symptomatology in the general population (Radloff 1977). The project uses the modified nine-item CES-D available in Add Health Wave III, consistent with prior Add Health work using the CES-D-9 (Desch et al. 2023).

The source items use a 0–3 response scale. Special response codes (6 = refused, 8 = don’t know, 9 = not applicable) are converted to system missing. Two positively worded items are reverse scored so that all nine items point in the same direction before the 0–27 sum score is created.

# ── Function: get weighted M and SD, and unweighted range ─────────────────────
get_desc <- function(var, design) {
  mn  <- as.numeric(svymean(as.formula(paste0("~", var)), design, na.rm = TRUE))
  vr  <- as.numeric(svyvar(as.formula(paste0("~", var)),  design, na.rm = TRUE))
  rng <- range(design$variables[[var]], na.rm = TRUE)
  data.frame(
    M     = formatC(mn,       digits = 2, format = "f"),
    SD    = formatC(sqrt(vr), digits = 2, format = "f"),
    Range = paste0(formatC(rng[1], digits = 2, format = "f"),
                   "–",
                   formatC(rng[2], digits = 2, format = "f")))
}

# ── Build rows for each variable group ────────────────────────────────────────
make_desc_rows <- function(var_named_vec, design, section_label) {

    d <- get_desc(v, design)
    data.frame(Variable = var_named_vec[[v]], M = d$M, SD = d$SD, Range = d$Range)
  })
  rows <- do.call(rbind, rows)
  rbind(data.frame(Variable = section_label, M = "", SD = "", Range = ""), rows)

Step 0: Build Outcome-Specific Disadvantage Composites

Analytic Rationale

A simple cumulative-risk index would assign equal weight to each disadvantage indicator. Here, the six indicators showed meaningfully different associations with depression, substance use, educational attainment, and general health. I therefore used outcome-specific beta-weighted composites, allowing each indicator’s contribution to reflect its empirical association with the outcome being modeled.

Race/ethnicity is included here as a marker of exposure to structural inequity within the original SPR Cup operationalization, not as an individual deficit or intrinsic risk characteristic.

For each outcome, the same survey-weighted regression is fit separately for minority status, school disadvantage, economic hardship, family socioeconomic disadvantage, subjective neighborhood disadvantage, and census-based neighborhood disadvantage. Rather than repeat 24 nearly identical code blocks, the workflow is parameterized and iterated below.

# Define `fit_disadvantage_model()` once so the same operation can be applied consistently across variables/models without duplicating code
fit_disadvantage_model <- function(outcome, predictor, design) {
  svyglm(
    reformulate(predictor, response = outcome),
    design = design,
    family = gaussian()
  )
}

# Generate every required outcome-by-predictor combination before fitting the repeated models programmatically
weight_results <- crossing(outcome_specs, disadvantage_specs) |>
  mutate(
    model = map2(
      outcome, predictor,
      ~fit_disadvantage_model(.x, .y, design_w3)
    ),
    b = map2_dbl(
      model, predictor,
      ~unname(coef(.x)[.y])
    ),
    p = map2_dbl(
      model, predictor,
      ~{
        coef_table <- summary(.x)$coefficients
        unname(coef_table[.y, "Pr(>|t|)"])
      }
    )
  )

Structural Disadvantage Composites

For each outcome, the structural disadvantage composite is the weighted sum of the six indicators, using the standardized coefficient from Step 0 as the weight. This approach retains the same conceptual domains across outcomes while allowing their empirical contribution to differ.

To make interpretation consistent across outcomes, composites tied to positively coded outcomes (educational attainment and general health) are multiplied by -1 after weighting. Higher values therefore indicate greater structural disadvantage for all four composites.

The weighting strategy is deliberately outcome-specific. A disadvantage indicator with a stronger relationship to depression, for example, contributes more to the depression composite than to an outcome for which its association is weaker.

Interpretive note. The beta weights are estimated in the same analytic sample in which the composites are subsequently used. This makes the approach useful for the present analysis, but the magnitude of the weighted composite associations should not be treated as out-of-sample predictive performance. A future extension could cross-validate the weights or evaluate them in an independent sample.

Step 2: Residuals

For each outcome, the residual from its Step 1 model captures how much better or worse a young adult is doing than expected given their adolescent disadvantage exposure. Treating resilience continuously preserves meaningful variation in adaptation and avoids imposing an arbitrary threshold for classifying individuals as “resilient” or “vulnerable.”

Depression and substance-use residuals are reverse-coded (* -1) so that, consistent with education and health, higher values always indicate greater resilience.

weighted_tertiles <- function(var, design) {
  cut_points <- as.numeric(

The subgroup estimates are consistent with stronger associations under high disadvantage for community connectedness and self-efficacy, whereas self-esteem and parent-child relationship show associations in both disadvantage groups.

Community connectedness shows a similar association with substance-use resilience in both disadvantage groups. Parent-child relationship shows a larger coefficient in the high-disadvantage group than in the low-disadvantage group, a pattern consistent with moderation that is evaluated directly in the interaction models below.

Community integration, self-efficacy, and school connectedness show associations with educational resilience in both disadvantage groups. School connectedness has a somewhat larger coefficient in the high-disadvantage group; the interaction analysis below provides the appropriate test of whether that difference is statistically meaningful.

Optimism/hopefulness and community connectedness show associations with general-health resilience primarily in the high-disadvantage subsample, while self-esteem is associated with resilience in both groups. These patterns motivate the formal moderation tests below rather than serving as stand-alone evidence that subgroup effects differ.

Key Findings and Analytic Takeaways

This analysis demonstrates an end-to-end quantitative workflow: cleaning and harmonizing a large longitudinal secondary dataset, documenting measurement decisions, incorporating survey weights, constructing outcome-specific measures, fitting and summarizing multivariable models, and translating technical results into substantive developmental interpretation.

Two findings are especially important:

  1. Structural disadvantage is multidimensional and outcome-specific. The relative contribution of school, family, economic, and neighborhood disadvantage differs across young-adult outcomes.

  2. Resilience is also domain-specific. Individual, family, school, and community factors do not show the same pattern across depression, substance use, education, and general health.

# Project Overview

This quantitative work sample demonstrates an end-to-end secondary-data
analysis using the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult
Health (Add Health) [@harris2013]. The analysis was originally developed
for the 21st Annual Sloboda and Bukoski SPR Cup and was selected as one
of four national finalist projects presented at the Society for
Prevention Research 34th Annual Meeting [@marcus2026].

::: {.callout-note appearance="simple" icon="false"}
### Project at a Glance

**Research question:** Which individual, family, school, and community
factors are associated with better-than-expected young-adult outcomes
given structural disadvantage in adolescence?

**Data:** Add Health Waves I and III; analytic *N* = 4,882.

**Methods:** Secondary-data cleaning and harmonization; complex
survey-weighted regression; outcome-specific beta-weighted structural
disadvantage composites; residual-based continuous resilience scores;
multivariable regression; exploratory subgroup comparisons and formal
interaction tests.

**Tools:** R, SPSS, Quarto, Git/GitHub.

**My role:** Led the analytic design, operationalization, statistical
analysis, interpretation, visualization, and reproducible documentation
as part of a multidisciplinary research team.
:::

The analysis proceeds in the following steps:

[Step 0](#sec-step0): Wave I structural disadvantage indicators are used to build
**outcome-specific disadvantage composites** [@marquez2023].

[Step 1](#sec-step1): Wave III outcomes are **regressed on the corresponding
disadvantage composites** [@miller-lewis2013].

[Step 2](#sec-step2): Standardized residuals from those models become **continuous
resilience scores**: the degree to which a young adult is doing better
or worse than expected given their adolescent disadvantage exposure.

[Step 3](#sec-step3): Multilevel resilience factors (individual, family, school,
and community) are tested as **predictors of resilience** in the full
sample.

[Step 4](#sec-step4): Exploratory low- and high-disadvantage subgroup regressions
to evaluate whether resilience factors are protective or promotive.

### Explicit Data Cleaning and Missing-Data Handling Example

Across the SPSS preprocessing files, source-variable frequencies were
inspected before recoding so that valid responses, study-specific
special codes, and legitimate skips could be handled explicitly rather
than treated as equivalent values.

Demographic covariates, including biological sex, age, and
race/ethnicity, were cleaned separately in `Demographics.sps` using the
same pattern of source-frequency checks, explicit recoding, variable
labels, and post-transformation validation.

The parental-employment component of family socioeconomic disadvantage
provides an example of this process.

For both the responding parent and their partner, the source variables
distinguish full-time employment (`1`), not employed full time (`0`),
refusal (`6`), and a legitimate skip for respondents who do not work
outside the home (`7`). Refusals are converted to system missing,
whereas legitimate skips are coded as not employed full time because
that response carries substantive information for the employment
indicator. Frequencies are checked before and after the transformation.
The two cleaned indicators are then averaged across available parents,
allowing the composite to retain information when employment data are
available for only one parent.

``` spss
frequencies variables = PA17 /statistics = all.

*7 = legitimate skip [does not work outside home].

if (PA17 eq 0) parentfulltimeD = 0.

if (PA17 eq 6) parentfulltimeD = -99.

    "Parent employed full time (PA17, 1=yes)".
value labels parentfulltimeD
    0 "not employed full time"
    1 "employed full time".
execute.
```

# Step 0: Build Outcome-Specific Disadvantage Composites {#sec-step0}

## Analytic Rationale

A simple cumulative-risk index would assign equal weight to each
disadvantage indicator. Here, the six indicators showed meaningfully
different associations with depression, substance use, educational
attainment, and general health. I therefore used **outcome-specific
beta-weighted composites**, allowing each indicator's contribution to
reflect its empirical association with the outcome being modeled.

Race/ethnicity is included here as a marker of exposure to structural
inequity within the original SPR Cup operationalization, not as an
individual deficit or intrinsic risk characteristic.

For each outcome, the same survey-weighted regression is fit separately
for minority status, school disadvantage, economic hardship, family
socioeconomic disadvantage, subjective neighborhood disadvantage, and
census-based neighborhood disadvantage. Rather than repeat 24 nearly
identical code blocks, the workflow is parameterized and iterated below.

```{r}

  ~outcome,          ~outcome_label,

  "Zgenhealth_W3",   "Health"

# Define `fit_disadvantage_model()` once so the same operation can be applied consistently across variables/models without duplicating code
fit_disadvantage_model <- function(outcome, predictor, design) {
  svyglm(
    reformulate(predictor, response = outcome),
    design = design,
    family = gaussian()
  )
}

# Generate every required outcome-by-predictor combination before fitting the repeated models programmatically
weight_results <- crossing(outcome_specs, disadvantage_specs) |>
  mutate(
    model = map2(
      outcome, predictor,
      ~fit_disadvantage_model(.x, .y, design_w3)
    ),
    b = map2_dbl(
      model, predictor,
      ~unname(coef(.x)[.y])
    ),
    p = map2_dbl(
      model, predictor,
      ~{
        coef_table <- summary(.x)$coefficients
        unname(coef_table[.y, "Pr(>|t|)"])
      }
    )
  )
```

This structure makes the repeated analysis auditable while reducing
copy-paste risk: the model specification is defined once and applied
consistently across all 24 predictor-outcome combinations.

## Structural Disadvantage Composites {#cumulative-adversity-composites}

For each outcome, the structural disadvantage composite is the weighted
sum of the six indicators, using the standardized coefficient from Step
0 as the weight. This approach retains the same conceptual domains
across outcomes while allowing their empirical contribution to differ.

To make interpretation consistent across outcomes, composites tied to
positively coded outcomes (educational attainment and general health)
are multiplied by `-1` after weighting. **Higher values therefore
indicate greater structural disadvantage for all four composites.**

::: {.callout-important appearance="minimal" icon="false"}
The weighting strategy is deliberately outcome-specific. A disadvantage
indicator with a stronger relationship to depression, for example,
contributes more to the depression composite than to an outcome for
which its association is weaker.
:::

::: {.callout-note appearance="simple" icon="false"}
**Interpretive note.** The beta weights are estimated in the same
analytic sample in which the composites are subsequently used. This
makes the approach useful for the present analysis, but the magnitude of
the weighted composite associations should not be treated as
out-of-sample predictive performance. A future extension could
cross-validate the weights or evaluate them in an independent sample.
:::

# Step 2: Residuals {#sec-step2}

For each outcome, the residual from its Step 1 model captures how much
better or worse a young adult is doing than expected given their
adolescent disadvantage exposure. Treating resilience continuously
preserves meaningful variation in adaptation and avoids imposing an
arbitrary threshold for classifying individuals as "resilient" or
"vulnerable."

Depression and substance-use residuals are reverse-coded (`* -1`) so
that, consistent with education and health, **higher values always
indicate greater resilience**.

```{r}
#| label: residuals
# ── Depression ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

# Extract the fitted regression coefficient for reuse in the next analytic step
dep_Zcumadv <- coef(model_depression)[2]

dat_w3$predicted_depression <- dep_int + dep_Zcumadv * dat_w3$Zcumadv_depression
dat_w3$residual_depression  <- dat_w3$ZcesD_sum - dat_w3$predicted_depression
# Flip: higher = more resilient
dat_w3$residual_depressionR <- dat_w3$residual_depression * -1
```

## Cross-Outcome Interpretation

The pattern is outcome-specific rather than uniformly protective across
domains. Self-esteem is most prominent for depression and general-health
resilience; parent-child relationship is strongest for substance-use
resilience; and school connectedness is especially important for
educational resilience. This heterogeneity is substantively useful:
factors associated with doing better than expected in one developmental
domain are not necessarily the strongest correlates in another.

**Create survey-weighted low-, middle-, and high-disadvantage groups**

For subgroup estimates, tertile cut points are calculated from the

```{r}
#| label: disadvantage-splits

# Define `weighted_tertiles()` once so the same operation can be applied consistently across variables/models without duplicating code
weighted_tertiles <- function(var, design) {
  cut_points <- as.numeric(
    coef(
      svyquantile(
        as.formula(paste0("~", var)),
        design,
        quantiles = c(.33, .66),
        ci = FALSE,
        na.rm = TRUE
      )
    )
  )

  cut(
    design$variables[[var]],
    breaks = c(-Inf, cut_points, Inf),
    labels = c("lo", "mid", "hi"),
    include.lowest = TRUE
  )
}

dat_w3$disadvantage_depression <-
  weighted_tertiles("Zcumadv_depression", design_w3)
dat_w3$disadvantage_substance <-
  weighted_tertiles("Zcumadv_substance", design_w3)
dat_w3$disadvantage_education <-
  weighted_tertiles("Zcumadv_education", design_w3)
dat_w3$disadvantage_health <-
  weighted_tertiles("Zcumadv_health", design_w3)
```

```{r}
#| label: table helper function
# Create function to make comparison table
make_protect_table <- function(model_full, model_lo, model_hi, outcome_label) {
  
  protective_vars <- c(
    "Zcommconnect", "Zcommintegr_disadv",
    "Zopt_hopeful", "Zselfesteem", "Zself_efficacy",
    "Zparentchild_rel",
    "Zschoolconnect"
  )
  
  var_labels <- c(
    "Zcommconnect"       = "Community Connectedness",
    "Zcommintegr_disadv" = "Community Integration",
    "Zopt_hopeful"       = "Optimism / Hopefulness",
    "Zselfesteem"        = "Self-Esteem",
    "Zself_efficacy"     = "Self-Efficacy",
    "Zparentchild_rel"   = "Parent-Child Relationship",
    "Zschoolconnect"     = "School Connectedness"
  )
  
  fmt_coef <- function(model, var) {
    coefs <- summary(model)$coefficients
    if (!var %in% rownames(coefs)) return("—")
    b <- coefs[var, "Estimate"]
    p <- coefs[var, "Pr(>|t|)"]
    stars <- case_when(
      p < .001 ~ "***",
      p < .01  ~ "**",
      p < .05  ~ "*",
      p < .10  ~ "^",
      TRUE     ~ ""
    )
    paste0(formatC(b, digits = 2, format = "f"), stars)
  }
  
  tbl <- data.frame(
    Variable       = var_labels[protective_vars],
    Full_Sample    = sapply(protective_vars, \(v) fmt_coef(model_full, v)),
    Low_Disadvantage  = sapply(protective_vars, \(v) fmt_coef(model_lo,   v)),
    High_Disadvantage = sapply(protective_vars, \(v) fmt_coef(model_hi,   v)),
    row.names = NULL
  )
  
  flextable(tbl) |>
    set_header_labels(
      Variable       = "Protective Factor",
      Full_Sample    = "Full Sample",
      Low_Disadvantage  = "Low Disadvantage",
      High_Disadvantage = "High Disadvantage"
    ) |>
    add_header_row(
      values    = c("", outcome_label),
      colwidths = c(1, 3)
    ) |>
    theme_apa() |>
    font(fontname = "Times New Roman", part = "all") |>
    fontsize(size = 11, part = "all") |>
    bold(part = "header") |>
    width(j = 1, width = 2.2) |>
    width(j = 2:4, width = 1.5) |>
    align(j = 2:4, align = "center", part = "all") |>
    add_footer_lines("^ p < .10. * p < .05. ** p < .01. *** p < .001.") |>
    fontsize(size = 9, part = "footer") |>
    italic(part = "footer")
}
```

# Key Findings and Analytic Takeaways

This analysis demonstrates an end-to-end quantitative workflow: cleaning
and harmonizing a large longitudinal secondary dataset, documenting
measurement decisions, incorporating survey weights, constructing
outcome-specific measures, fitting and summarizing multivariable models,
and translating technical results into substantive developmental
interpretation.

Two findings are especially important:

1.  **Structural disadvantage is multidimensional and
    outcome-specific.** The relative contribution of school, family,
    economic, and neighborhood disadvantage differs across young-adult
    outcomes.

2.  **Resilience is also domain-specific.** Individual, family, school,
    and community factors do not show the same pattern across
    depression, substance use, education, and general health.
